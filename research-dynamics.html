Planetary systems remember their past.

A planet’s orbit is not just where it happens to be today. Its shape and tilt preserve evidence of migration, close encounters, and the influence of neighboring planets. By comparing a planet’s orbital path with the rotation of its star, we can test how quietly—or chaotically—the system evolved.

I work on reconstructing the histories of common super-Earth and sub-Neptune systems. These planets have no direct counterpart in our Solar System, so their orbital architectures help us understand the many different ways planetary systems can form.

A shadow with a telltale distortion

As a planet moves across a rotating star, it first blocks one side of the star and then the other. That creates a small, temporary change in the measured stellar spectrum. The pattern reveals whether the planet’s orbit is aligned with the star’s equator.

Constraining formation histories with stellar obliquity

The projected spin-orbit angle provides a fossil record of migration and dynamical excitation. I work on measuring the sky-projected obliquities of super-Earth and sub-Neptune systems through the Rossiter-McLaughlin effect: an anomalous radial-velocity signal generated as a transiting planet successively occults the approaching and receding stellar hemispheres.

Placed in the context of multiplicity and system architecture, these measurements distinguish dynamically quiescent pathways from histories involving secular interactions, scattering, or disk-driven migration.

Population context

My work contributes to RODEO (Rossiter Observations Determining Exoplanet Obliquities) and ACES (Architecture of Compact Exoplanetary Systems), collaborative efforts to interpret individual systems within the broader population.
