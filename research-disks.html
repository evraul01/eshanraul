What ingredients are available when rocky planets form?

Young stars are surrounded by disks of gas and dust. Inside them, grains collide and grow into planets while heat, radiation, and chemistry continually reshape the available material. The molecules in a disk therefore influence what newborn planets—and eventually their atmospheres—can be made from.

I study carbon-bearing and organic molecules in the warm inner disk, where terrestrial planets assemble. JWST can now detect this chemistry with far more detail than was previously possible.

Carbon grains as chemical fuel

One part of my work models what happens when solid carbon grains are destroyed and their carbon returns to the gas. That supply can drive rich hydrocarbon chemistry and alter the balance of carbon and oxygen inherited by forming planets.

Tracing volatile evolution in terrestrial-planet-forming zones

Mid-infrared molecular emission probes the gas composition of the warm inner regions of protoplanetary disks. I combine chemical modeling with JWST/MIRI spectral interpretation to constrain water, hydrocarbons, and complex organic molecules in these regions.

My first-author modeling work investigated the chemical response to carbon grain destruction, showing how enhanced carbon supply can raise the gas-phase C/O ratio and generate hydrocarbon-rich inner-disk chemistry. My current observational work extends this question to evolved Upper Scorpius disks and the broader JDISCS sample.

From inner disks to planetary inventories

By connecting inner- and outer-disk physics and chemistry, we can better estimate the volatile reservoirs and organic feedstock available during rocky-planet assembly.
