01 Research

From planetary origins
to other worlds.

I study how planets form, how their orbits change, and what their atmospheres are made of—three connected pieces of the search for worlds that could harbor life.

I connect protoplanetary-disk astrochemistry, dynamical architecture, and atmospheric characterization to understand the formation and evolution of terrestrial planets and sub-Neptunes.

Explanation level
01 / Primary focus

Exoplanet atmospheres & retrievals

I use JWST observations to read the chemical fingerprints in exoplanet atmospheres and learn about clouds, water, and the conditions on distant worlds.

I analyze transmission spectra and population-level JWST archival data, combining atmospheric retrievals and machine learning to investigate composition and cloud trends in super-Earths and sub-Neptunes.

02 / System architecture

Exoplanet dynamics

A planet’s orbit preserves clues about its past. I use those clues to work out how common planetary systems moved and changed.

I investigate dynamical histories and spin-orbit architectures of super-Earth and sub-Neptune systems, including Rossiter-McLaughlin measurements of projected obliquity.

03 / Planet formation

Protoplanetary disk chemistry

Before planets exist, gas and dust orbit young stars. I trace water, carbon, and organic molecules in the warm inner regions where rocky planets begin.

I model hydrocarbon evolution and interpret JWST/MIRI spectra to constrain volatile and complex-organic abundances in terrestrial-planet-forming regions of protoplanetary disks.

02 Scientific motivation

A connected planetary history.

Planets do not begin with atmospheres and orbits already set. They grow inside disks, interact with other bodies, migrate, lose material, and build or transform their atmospheres. By studying several stages of that history, I want to understand which paths can produce a terrestrial planet capable of supporting life.

JWST gives us an unprecedented way to test those ideas: it can measure molecules in both planet-forming disks and exoplanet atmospheres.

Atmospheric composition, system architecture, and natal-disk chemistry are coupled observables of planetary formation and evolution. My work uses spectroscopy, physical modeling, dynamical inference, and population-level analysis to connect those observables.

The long-term goal is to identify robust pathways to temperate terrestrial planets and develop the characterization methods needed to assess their habitability.
