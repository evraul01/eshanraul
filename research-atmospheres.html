What can light tell us about an atmosphere?

When an exoplanet passes in front of its star, a small amount of starlight filters through the planet’s atmosphere. Molecules absorb particular colors, leaving a pattern in the data. By comparing that pattern with atmospheric models, we can estimate what gases are present and whether clouds are hiding part of the signal.

I use this approach to study super-Earths and sub-Neptunes—planets that are common around other stars but absent from our own Solar System. Understanding them is an essential step toward interpreting smaller, potentially habitable worlds.

My current question

How does cloudiness change across the population of super-Earths and sub-Neptunes? I am using the growing JWST archive and machine learning to look for trends that individual planets cannot reveal on their own.

Inferring atmospheric composition from transmission spectra

During transit, wavelength-dependent changes in planetary radius encode absorption and scattering by atmospheric constituents. Atmospheric retrieval frameworks compare forward models with the measured transmission spectrum to infer molecular abundances, thermal structure, aerosol properties, and related parameters.

My dissertation work investigates population-level trends in cloud presence across super-Earths and sub-Neptunes using JWST archival spectra and machine-learning methods. The aim is to identify empirical structure in aerosol behavior and improve the physical context in which individual retrievals are interpreted.

Water worlds & volatile-rich planets

As a member of a large JWST Cycle 2 program, I have worked on atmospheric characterization of potential water worlds, including the water-rich “steam world” GJ 9827 d and the high-metallicity sub-Neptune GJ 3090 b.
